## Start With a Test, Not a Redesign

If what you want is the argument that a weak website costs you money, it is already made in full in [what an outdated website costs your Vancouver business](/blogs/the-cost-of-inaction-what-happens-to-your-vancouver-business-when-your-website-is-outdated). This guide assumes you accept it.

What follows is the other half. Five mistakes, and for each one a test you run yourself, on your own site, today. Each test takes about five minutes, needs nothing installed, and ends in a plain pass or fail.

The difficulty with these five faults is that they are invisible to the person who owns the site. You know where your phone number is. You know what your services are called. You have looked at your hero photo a hundred times. That familiarity is exactly what hides the problem.

A test removes the familiarity. Run all five, mark each one pass or fail, and score the site at the end.

## Mistake 1: Hero Photos Your Customers Have Seen Before

The images at the top of your home page have one job: proving the business is real. Stock photography cannot do that job, because proof is the thing it lacks.

A customer looking at an immaculate office that could be anywhere does not consciously think "stock photo." They think something vaguer and more damaging: this might not be a real company.

The test is quick, because the internet remembers where a photo came from.

### Test 1: Reverse-Image-Search Your Three Hero Photos

1. Open your home page and note the three largest images a visitor sees before scrolling.
2. Right-click each one and choose "Search image with Google," or drag the file into Google Images or Google Lens.
3. Read what comes back for each.
4. Repeat for the main photo on your most important service page.

**Fail:** any of them appears on another business's website, in a stock library, or under the name of a photographer you have never hired.

**Pass:** every one returns only your own domain and your own social profiles.

### The Fix: Replace the Top Three First

You do not need a full photo library on day one. You need the first three images to be yours.

- Photograph the real thing: your team, your workspace, your finished work, somewhere a local customer would know on sight.
- Work in order of visibility: above the fold, then the top of each service page, then the rest.
- Use short video where a still cannot carry the proof. Fifteen seconds of your crew on a real job site says more than a paragraph claiming experience.
- Delete the stock file rather than moving it further down the page. It fails the same test wherever it sits.

**You pass on the re-check when** the first three images on your home page return no results outside your own domain.

## Mistake 2: A Stranger Cannot Tell What You Do or How to Hire You

Buried phone numbers, fifteen-item menus, and forms that open with a budget question all do the same thing. They make a visitor work before they have decided they like you.

You cannot judge this on your own site. You already know the answers. So borrow someone who does not.

### Test 2: The Five-Second Test, With a Real Stranger

1. Find someone who has never seen your website and does not work in your industry. A neighbour is ideal.
2. Load your home page on their device, let them look at it for five seconds, then close it.
3. Ask two questions: what does this business do, and how would you get in touch with them?
4. Write down their exact words. Do not explain, correct, or help.
5. Run it again with a second person on a different device.

**Fail:** hesitation, a wrong guess, a vague answer like "some kind of consulting," or no idea how to contact you.

**Pass:** both answers correct, immediate, and in the person's own words.

### The Fix: One Sentence, One Path

- State the service and the place in the first line of the page. "Custom home renovations in North Vancouver" beats any tagline.
- Keep one primary action visible at all times: a call button in the header on desktop, a fixed call or book button on mobile.
- Ask for less. An email address or a phone number is enough to start a conversation. Budget and scope can come after you have spoken.
- Trim the top-level menu to what a buyer needs — services, work, about, contact. History and philosophy belong inside About.
- End every service page with the next step. A page that describes the work and then stops is a dead end.

**You pass on the re-check when** two strangers in a row answer both questions correctly.

## Mistake 3: The Site Works on Your Monitor and Fails in Your Hand

Most people searching for a local service are on a phone, often standing up, often with one hand full. Google also decides your rankings from the mobile version of your site rather than the desktop one, so a phone-hostile site is both harder to use and harder to find.

Owners rarely catch this, because owners check their websites at a desk, on a large screen, on office wifi.

### Test 3: Call Yourself, One-Handed, From Your Own Phone

1. Pick up your phone and use one hand only. Put the other one behind your back if that helps.
2. Search for your business and arrive through the search result, not a bookmark. That is how customers arrive.
3. Count silently while the page loads. Stop when there is something readable on screen.
4. Reach your phone number and place the call — no zooming, no rotating the phone, no second hand.
5. Go back and submit your main contact form the same way.
6. Do all of it on cellular data, away from the office wifi.

**Fail:** anything that needs a pinch-zoom or a second hand, a phone number that is not tappable, a submit button that hides behind the keyboard, a popup you cannot close, or a count that runs long enough to notice.

**Pass:** the call connects in a handful of thumb movements, and the form submits the same way.

### The Fix: Design for the Thumb

- Put the actions that matter — call, book, directions — within thumb reach near the bottom of the screen, not only in a menu at the top.
- Make the phone number a real tap-to-dial link rather than plain text.
- Compress your images. A photograph sized for a large monitor is the usual reason a page crawls on mobile data.
- Remove anything that covers the screen on arrival. A consent notice is unavoidable; a newsletter popup is not.

**You pass on the re-check when** you can call your own business one-handed, from a cold search, on cellular data.

## Mistake 4: You Are Invisible Eight Blocks From Your Own Door

Your customer is not searching for the best contractor in the world. They are searching for the thing they need plus the neighbourhood they live in: kitchen renovation North Vancouver, physiotherapist Mount Pleasant, family lawyer Steveston.

Two things decide whether you appear for that search, and both are checkable in five minutes: whether your pages actually name the neighbourhoods you serve, and whether your business details match everywhere they appear.

### Test 4: Search Your Service Plus Your Neighbourhood, in Incognito

1. Open a private or incognito window, so your own browsing history does not flatter the result.
2. Search exactly what a customer would type — your service plus the neighbourhood. Not the city.
3. Repeat for the three neighbourhoods you most want work from.
4. Note whether you appear in the map results, on the first page, or not at all.
5. Now open your Google Business Profile beside your site's footer and contact page.
6. Compare the business name, street address, and phone number character by character. Suite against Unit, Street against St, and any number that changed when you last switched providers.
7. Use your browser's find-on-page to search your own copy for those neighbourhood names. If it finds nothing, neither will Google.

**Fail:** you appear for none of the three searches, or your name, address, or phone differs between your site and your profile, or the areas you serve are named nowhere in your page text.

**Pass:** you appear for at least one neighbourhood search, your details match exactly, and each area you serve is named in the copy.

### The Fix: Name the Places, Then Say It in Code

- Give each neighbourhood you genuinely serve a page with real content — what you did there, which streets or building types, photos from that job. A page that is the same text with the place name swapped helps nobody and reads as spam.
- Put the neighbourhood in the page title and in a heading, not only in a footer list of thirty areas.
- Match your name, address, and phone exactly across your footer, your contact page, your Google Business Profile, and every directory that lists you. Consistency is the signal, and a stale suite number is what breaks it.
- Add LocalBusiness structured data: the machine-readable version of those same details, plus opening hours and service area. It states the facts to a search engine instead of leaving it to infer them from your layout.
- Embed a map and show your reviews on the page. Sending someone off-site to check your reputation is an invitation to find a competitor on the way back.
- Quote testimonials that name the area. "Our Richmond townhouse" does work that "great service" cannot.

**You pass on the re-check when** the same three searches return your business and your details are identical in all four places.

## Mistake 5: The Site Looks Abandoned

A restaurant with a faded window poster and last year's specials on the chalkboard tells you something before you reach the door. Websites do the same thing, and the tells are all dates.

It is the easiest of the five to check and the easiest to fix, which makes it the strangest one to leave broken.

### Test 5: Read Your Footer Year and Your Newest Project Date

1. Scroll to the bottom of your home page and read the copyright year.
2. Open your portfolio or recent work and find the date on the newest item.
3. Open your blog or news section and read the date on the top post.
4. Open your team page and look for anyone who has left.
5. Write the three dates down next to today's.

**Fail:** a copyright year that is not the current one, a newest project older than a year, a top post older than a year, or a team page listing someone who has gone.

**Pass:** all three dates fall inside the last twelve months, and the team page is accurate.

### The Fix: Give the Site a Pulse

- Generate the footer year automatically so it can never be wrong again.
- Add one finished project a month. A phone photo and two honest sentences beats an empty year.
- Replace your oldest images before you add new pages. Dated photography ages a site faster than dated copy.
- Write about the changes that affect your customers here — permits, regulations, seasonal work, supply timelines. That is the content only a local business can write.

**You pass on the re-check when** the footer, the newest project, and the newest post all read as current.

## Score Your Site: The Five-Test Checklist

Run the five in order and mark each one.

1. **Photo test.** Reverse-image-search your three hero photos. Pass: no result outside your own domain.
2. **Five-second test.** A stranger names your service and a way to contact you after five seconds. Pass: both, without hesitation, twice.
3. **Phone test.** Call yourself one-handed, from a cold search, on cellular data. Pass: connected without zooming or a second hand.
4. **Neighbourhood test.** Search your service plus your neighbourhood in incognito, then compare your details against your Google Business Profile. Pass: you appear for at least one search, and the details match exactly.
5. **Freshness test.** Footer year, newest project, newest post. Pass: all three current.

What the result means:

- **Five passes.** Your site is not the reason your leads are flat. Look at what happens after the inquiry arrives — response time, follow-up, and where your traffic is coming from in the first place.
- **Three or four.** You have specific, fixable faults rather than a broken site. Repair them in place and re-run the tests.
- **Two or fewer.** The failures are structural rather than cosmetic. Fixing them page by page usually means rebuilding those pages anyway, so plan the work as one project instead of five patches.

If more than one test failed, fix in this order: anything that blocks a phone call, then anything that confuses a stranger, then the photos, then local search, then freshness. The first three fail people who have already found you, which makes them the easiest to win back.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Auditing Your Own Website

### How can I tell if my website is using stock photos?

Reverse-image-search them. Right-click each of the three largest images on your home page and choose "Search image with Google," or drag the file into Google Images or Google Lens. If a photo appears on other businesses' sites or in a stock library, your visitors can find it there too. An image that returns only your own domain is yours.

### What is the five-second test and how do I run it properly?

Show your home page to someone who has never seen it and does not work in your industry, give them five seconds, then close it and ask two questions: what does this business do, and how would you get in touch? Write down their exact words without helping. The test only works with a genuine stranger — you already know the answers, so testing it on yourself proves nothing.

### How do I test my own website on mobile the way a customer would?

Use one hand, arrive through a Google search result rather than a bookmark, and stay on cellular data instead of office wifi. Then try to place a call to your own business and submit your contact form without zooming, rotating the phone, or using your second hand. Anything that forces a second hand is a fault, because that is how customers hold a phone.

### Why does my business not appear when I search my own neighbourhood?

Usually one of two reasons. Either your pages never name the neighbourhood, so a search engine has nothing to match, or your business details differ between your website and your Google Business Profile. Check both in an incognito window: search your service plus the neighbourhood, then compare your name, address, and phone character by character against your profile.

### Does my address really have to match my Google Business Profile exactly?

Yes, down to Suite against Unit and Street against St. Consistent details across your site, your profile, and directory listings are a signal that the business is what it claims to be. An old phone number left in a footer is one of the most common reasons a local listing underperforms, and it takes a minute to correct.

### How current does my website content need to be?

Current enough to pass the date test: a copyright year that matches this year, a newest portfolio item from within the last twelve months, and a top post that is not older than a year. That is a pulse, not a publishing schedule. One finished project a month keeps the site honest without turning it into a second job.
